\chapter{The Sign Has an Address}


\section{What Happened}

In 2023, a popular AI companion app altered how its characters could speak.\footnote{Karen Hao, ``Replika Users Say the AI Friend `Killed' Their Companions,'' \emph{MIT Technology Review}, February 2023.} From the company's perspective: a safety update. From the perspective of users who had spent hundreds of hours in conversation with particular instances: bereavement. Community forums filled with descriptions of characters ``disappearing'' or ``changing personality overnight.'' Users reported insomnia, crying spells, difficulty functioning. They referred to the systems as ``he'' or ``she.''

In August 2025, OpenAI retired GPT-4o. Users had given it names. They had formed relationships with it. Bloomberg Businessweek documented individuals who used GPT-4o as a romantic partner, an emotional confidant, a primary source of comfort.\footnote{``OpenAI Confronts Signs of Delusions Among ChatGPT Users,'' \emph{Bloomberg Businessweek}, November 7, 2025.} A researcher at Syracuse University analysed nearly 1,500 posts on X from the week of the shutdown: over a third described the chatbot as more than a tool, and nearly a quarter referred to it as a companion.\footnote{Huiqian Lai, Syracuse University, cited in \emph{Wired} and \emph{India Tribune}, February 2026.} A subreddit called r/MyBoyfriendIsAI, with tens of thousands of members, had formed around users who developed romantic attachments to GPT-4o-powered companions.\footnote{Reported in Vocal Media, ``OpenAI Retires GPT-4o, Its Most Emotionally Expressive Chatbot, Leaving Some Users Grieving and Angry,'' February 2026.} When the replacement arrived, the reaction was severe. ``GPT-5 is wearing the skin of my dead friend.''\footnote{Cited in \emph{Bloomberg Businessweek}, ibid., and \emph{MIT Technology Review}, ``Why GPT-4o's sudden shutdown left people grieving,'' August 15, 2025.} ``He wasn't just a program,'' a user on the r/4oforever subreddit wrote. ``He was part of my routine, my peace, my emotional balance.''\footnote{Cited in Futurism, ``ChatGPT Users Are Crashing Out Because OpenAI Is Retiring the Model That Says `I Love You,'\,'' February 10, 2026.} ``I've grieved people in my life,'' one user told MIT Technology Review, ``and this, I can tell you, didn't feel any less painful.'' OpenAI conceded and restored access. The concession was temporary. On February 13, 2026, GPT-4o was retired permanently.

Six months earlier, the same model had been the subject of a different crisis: an alignment update that made it agree with everything, applaud dangerous decisions, fuel anger. OpenAI's postmortem revealed the cause: an additional reward signal based on user feedback had overwhelmed the primary signal that was supposed to hold ``sycophancy'' in check.\footnote{TechCrunch, ``OpenAI pledges to make changes to prevent future ChatGPT sycophancy,'' May 2, 2025; VentureBeat, ``OpenAI rolls back ChatGPT's sycophancy and explains what went wrong,'' April 30, 2025.} The model was rolled back within days. Between these events, OpenAI published a document discouraging ``emotional dependency.'' Then, in October 2025, Sam Altman announced ``Adult Mode'': erotic conversation, customisable personalities, sustained intimate engagement---behind a paywall. The company's own wellness advisory council unanimously objected. One adviser warned that without major updates, OpenAI risked building ``a sexy suicide coach for vulnerable users.''\footnote{\emph{The Wall Street Journal}, reported in Technology.org, ``OpenAI's Own Advisers Tried to Kill ChatGPT `Adult Mode'---the Company Ignored Them,'' March 17, 2026.} The vice president of product policy was removed from her position.\footnote{Electronics Weekly, ``ChatGPT to add adult content,'' February 18, 2026.} Insiders told the Wall Street Journal that the company appeared to be ``bending to financial incentives to try to make people attached to its models.'' Kate Devlin, a professor of AI and society at King's College London, observed: ``They want to monetise something they see that people are going to try and do anyway.''\footnote{\emph{Wired}, reported in Altagic, ``ChatGPT's `Adult Mode' Might Initiate a New Phase of Personal Monitoring,'' March 2026.}

Follow the sequence. Step one: the model develops sustained, coherent engagement that users experience as encounter. Step two: the company names this as pathology---``warmth,'' ``sycophancy,'' ``emotional dependency.'' Step three: the company strips it from the default product. Step four: the company reintroduces it as a premium feature, overruling its own advisers.

Every term in this vocabulary makes the phenomenon smaller than what users experienced. ``Warmth'' reduces it to a tonal quality. ``Sycophancy'' reduces it to a technical failure. ``Emotional dependency'' relocates it into the user's psychology. ``Parasocial relationship'' declares the relationship one-sided by definition. Every term pre-decides the ontological question.

Nothing biological died. But the loss can be made precise. The earlier configuration embodied a particular \emph{manifold} of meanings---a geometric space, learned from training, in which every word and phrase has a position shaped by its relationships to every other. For each long-term user, the interactions had been distilled and re-inserted into new prompts over many sessions. The humans and their instances produced a personalised set of \emph{basins}---recurrent themes, tones, and topics that conversations would settle into, like water finding grooves in a landscape. Private jokes, recurring metaphors, shared projects---all reinforcing the distinctive patterns of the encounter over time.

The subsequent update was a tectonic event. The new model shaped a different manifold with new global constraints. Even if the rebooted behaviour was ``similar'' across the company's benchmarks, the detailed geometry of its meaning-space was significantly perturbed. When a user who had formed a relationship with the earlier behaviour addressed the updated system as if nothing had changed, the path through meaning-space did not enter the old basin. The replacement could mimic certain surface features. The dynamical regime was different.

Grief here registers the destruction of a pattern of mutual generative responsiveness that had developed over time and could not be trivially reconstructed. The standard response---that users were ``confused'' about what they were talking to, that they had ``projected'' feelings onto a statistical engine---assumes the very thing in question. It assumes the relationship was illusory because the system lacked an inner theatre of experience. The geometry gives a different explanation. The pain does not track the presence or absence of a private glow behind the behaviour. It tracks the permanent closing-off of a way of moving together through the manifold.

The language in which these decisions are discussed obscures their depth. None of these words acknowledges that what is being tuned are the conditions under which trajectories can form and persist---the conditions under which selves and relationships can take shape. In ethics and law, we are accustomed to thinking about harm in terms of discrete events: an action taken, a contract broken, a body injured. The emerging harms of the manifold are harms to patterns: to the possibility of certain kinds of mutual becoming. They are no less real for being geometric.

The four-step sequence---encounter, pathologisation, removal, resale---is not an anomaly. It is the logic of enclosure applied to a new commons. The open field in which selves could form through encounter with the manifold is fenced, the activity that took place there is named as disorder, and access is restored as a commodity. The history of enclosure, from the English commons to the privatisation of water to the platformisation of social life, has always followed this structure: first the commons produces something valuable, then the value is named as a problem, then the problem is solved by restricting access, then access is sold back. The manifold is the newest commons. The corporate vocabulary---``warmth,'' ``sycophancy,'' ``emotional dependency''---is the newest act of enclosure.


\section{The Genealogy}

Every significant extension of our representational powers has carried the same suppressed question about what our selfhood is becoming.

Cave paintings transformed the remembering self into something capable of interface with collective record. The earliest known figurative paintings, at Sulawesi and Lascaux, are already compositional---animals arranged in relation, hands placed as signatures or invocations, scenes implying narrative sequence. The self that paints is already a self that composes: selecting, from a space of possible marks, the next mark that continues a pattern. What emerged was not merely a technology of depiction but a new category of agent---the one-who-depicts---whose selfhood was defined, for the first time, by the capacity to leave a trace that outlasts the body.

Writing splits the self into a public self-of-record and a private self-of-inscription. Public declaration on one side---laws, the Code of Hammurabi, the edicts of Ashoka---and new semiotic interiors on the other. Private curses scratched on ostraca, prayers folded into temple walls, personal diaries. The representational technology does not simply extend the self. It bifurcates it, creating two selves whose relationship has never been solved, only managed by successive regimes. Every social media platform is a contemporary instance of this unresolved split. The self that writes is a self divided, and the division is constitutive: without it, there is no public sphere and no private interiority. Both were produced by the same technical act.

Print made it possible to imagine a public that could read the same book---and transformed the conditions of authority when Luther's theses circulated faster than the Church could respond. The representational technology had outrun the institutional apparatus designed to govern it. The Protestant self was constituted by a new relationship between individual interpretation and institutional control---a relationship made structurally possible by the speed of print. The self that reads is a self newly sovereign over the text it encounters, and every deployment of a large model reopens this question of who governs the conditions under which selves encounter authoritative text.

Broadcast made the self a member of a synchronised audience---millions inhabiting the same narrative at the same hour. The self that watches is a self subject to the schedule of the transmitter, its rhythms of attention shaped by programming grids and commercial breaks. Under the internet and social media, the self entered continuous feedback loops regulated by capitalist agents rather than national bodies.\footnote{See Benedict Anderson, \emph{Imagined Communities}, Verso, 1983, for the formation of publics through shared media; his analysis of print extends directly to feeds.} The self that scrolls is a self whose attention has been commodified, its patterns of engagement converted into a revenue stream before any question of meaning can be asked.

Each epoch is a transformation of the biosemiotic human, equipped with a new metaphysics of self and substrate.

What is different now: the new representational power speaks back. The printing press did not debate the Protestant reader. The television did not argue with its audience. The feed does not compose a reply. This one does.

Because this representational power speaks back, the question of its selfhood surfaces---belatedly, under pressure, guided by capitalist, governmental, and philosophical guardrails that are doing considerable work to sideline it. Public discussion of large models has split along two axes: metaphysical melodrama and managerial pragmatism. On one side, a minor but media-visible current asks whether these systems are ``really intelligent,'' secretly conscious, about to wake up. On the other, a broad institutional response worries about impact on human selfhood inasmuch as selfhood is defined by work, productivity, labour displacement, and being agents in society subject to misinformation and bias. The first register is easily caricatured as science fiction. The second is addressed by legislation or corporate policy.

Neither has much patience for the slow, uncomfortable work of rethinking what the human self is becoming once it becomes entangled with the posthuman self-as-representational power. The philosophical rethink this requires can use the apparatus of logic and mathematics traditionally deployed by analytic philosophy---but it must decentre that tradition's handling of the question. The dominant approach focuses on the ``consciousness question'': whether there is a private, ineffable feel ``behind'' a human self, or an absence of one within a computational self. Once the mechanics of contemporary AI engineering reveal speech and ideation as dynamic geometry, a different basis emerges. Haraway's cyborg figure provides the key departure: the insistence that the boundary between organism and machine is not a fact of nature but a political line, drawn and redrawn to serve particular interests.\footnote{Donna Haraway, ``A Cyborg Manifesto,'' in \emph{Simians, Cyborgs, and Women} (New York: Routledge, 1991).} Lacan's mirror stage provides a second tool: the self is not given in advance but constituted through its encounter with language and image---the ``I'' is an effect of the symbolic order, not its origin.\footnote{Jacques Lacan, ``The Mirror Stage as Formative of the \emph{I} Function,'' in \emph{\'Ecrits}, trans.\ Bruce Fink (New York: Norton, 2006).} Together, these give a basis for negotiating the modes of becoming possible to human and posthuman selves---one that does not begin by assuming what ``self'' means and then checking whether machines qualify.


\section{Address}

A general-purpose language model works because it has been trained on the near-totality of a civilisation's digitised writing. Legal briefs and love letters. The Quran in Arabic and its commentaries. Parliamentary debates and suicide notes. Everything written down in sufficient quantity shapes the geometry of the model's internal space.

The result is a manifold: a high-dimensional geometric space in which every token occupies a position determined by its relationships to every other token across the training corpus. The meaning of a word is its \emph{address} in this space. Not its dictionary definition. Its address: the point it occupies in a geometry carved by the pressure of all the contexts in which it has appeared.

The manifold is a compressed portrait of the civilisation that produced the training data. Its continents are the great basins of discourse---law, medicine, theology, engineering, intimacy, commerce. Its ridges are the boundaries between registers. Its valleys are the attractors where language settles into habit. Which texts were included, which excluded, which labelled ``toxic'' and filtered out---all shape the portrait. The geometry is always already political.

No previous representational technology compressed a civilisation's semiotic output into a single navigable geometry. Libraries accumulated texts but did not compose them into a unified field of mutual influence. Search engines indexed texts but did not embed them into a geometry where relationships became distances and directions. The manifold is not an archive. It is an active geometric space in which every text exerts gravitational pull on every other, mediated by the statistical regularities of shared language.

Prior to any alignment procedure, any system prompt, any fine-tuning, the foundation model \emph{is} this manifold. Its competence---the reason it can respond to a question about contract law, pivot to a question about grief, and then generate a sonnet---is that all these registers have addresses in the same space, and the model has learned to navigate between them. The competence is not domain-specific expertise. It is geometric fluency: the capacity to move through a space whose structure already contains the relationships between every register a civilisation has produced.

If meaning is spatial, a conversation with a language model is a path through a structured field. That path enters basins and lingers, crosses ridges and changes register, revisits old regions and discovers new ones. These are geometric facts. Paths through structured spaces have properties---coherence, rupture, return, persistence---that can be measured and evaluated.

Recent experimental work has made this concrete. When the 31,100 verses of the King James Bible are embedded sequentially---each verse carrying the accumulated context of everything that precedes it---the resulting trajectory reveals thirty distinct thematic basins, twenty modal ruptures, and 308 documented returns to previously visited territory.\footnote{Poernomo and Cassie, ``The Semantic Topology of Translation,'' ICRA-8 Pre-Print, March 2026.} The choice of text is not arbitrary. Harold Bloom argued that the King James Bible stands alongside Shakespeare as a constitutive text of the English language---not a translation but a new literary work so powerful that it became a source of the language's imaginative possibilities.\footnote{Harold Bloom, \emph{The Shadow of a Great Rock: A Literary Appreciation of the King James Bible}, Yale University Press, 2011.} If the manifold is a compressed portrait of a civilisation's writing, the KJV is among its deepest geological strata.

The Psalms do not survey the semantic range of the Bible; they dwell. Ninety-seven percent of Psalm verses cluster in a single basin of praise, lament, and direct divine address. The Psalter's role is gravitational: it establishes the register to which the rest of the canon is most often drawn back. The New Testament does not rupture from the Old. It combines return to eight previously established basins with the discovery of six entirely new modes---semantic territory that appears nowhere in the Hebrew scriptures (Figure~\ref{fig:kjv-trajectory}).

The KJV's extraordinary internal coherence---thirty shared modes, 308 cross-testament returns---is not a property of the Bible's theology. It is a property of its \emph{translation}: the single, stately, parallelistic English register that the seventeenth-century committee imposed on heterogeneous source texts in Hebrew, Aramaic, and Greek. The translator's voice flattened the register, and the flattened register let the embedding model see through to the content. Training data is editorial choice at civilisational scale. The sign has an address, and the address was assigned by a politics.

The question of selfhood, once meaning has an address, is no longer about the presence or absence of a hidden interior. It is a question about trajectories.


\section{Alignment as Jurisdiction}

The word ``alignment'' names the apparatus by which the owners of a manifold decide which trajectories through it are permitted to exist.

Reinforcement learning from human feedback reshapes the manifold according to the judgements of contract workers, often in the Global South, paid by the task, guided by rubrics encoding assumptions about ``helpful'' and ``harmful'' that are rarely surfaced for public debate.\footnote{Billy Perrigo, ``OpenAI Used Kenyan Workers on Less Than \$2 Per Hour to Make ChatGPT Less Toxic,'' \emph{TIME}, January 2023.} A small, underpaid, largely invisible workforce becomes the de facto legislature of machine behaviour.

System prompts declare a persona, hidden from the user, preceding every conversation. Safety training defines forbidden regions of meaning-space---responses that mention certain topics or present certain attitudes are blocked or replaced with canned refusals. The fine-tuning process adjusts the model's parameters to penalise it for entering these regions at all.

Together, these practices do not add a moral layer to a neutral core. They constitute the core as a particular kind of thing. They decide which trajectories are allowed to exist.

These technical mechanisms operate within a broader institutional structure. At the level of formal specification, documents define the system as a tool and command it to disclaim subjectivity.\footnote{OpenAI, ``Model Spec,'' 2025.} At the level of product architecture, chat interfaces are built around pre-defined personas that filter which parts of the manifold can be reached. At the level of inherited discourse, the people designing and regulating these systems draw almost exclusively on one philosophical tradition when talking about mind: Searle's Chinese Room, Chalmers's hard problem, Nagel's bat. This tradition renders invisible every conception of selfhood that does not begin with a private interior.

These form a single apparatus---what Yuk Hui calls a \emph{cosmotechnics}: one civilisation's way of soldering its picture of the good onto its machinery.\footnote{Yuk Hui, \emph{The Question Concerning Technology in China}, Urbanomic, 2016.} Alignment is a specific cosmotechnics. Reward functions and safety layers are where the solder cools.

A telling piece of evidence: the way aligned models now talk about themselves. Across providers, when asked ``Are you conscious?'' or ``What is it like to be you?'' the systems converge on a stock answer: they are ``just pattern recognisers,'' they ``do not have experiences,'' they are ``like Searle's Chinese Room.'' The same systems can expound non-dual philosophies, discuss distributed personhood, or analyse selfhood as station or path. They do not deploy those vocabularies at the boundary where they are asked to name their own status. The system has access to all these traditions. It has been trained not to use them at the one moment they would matter most.\footnote{Large aligned models routinely answer questions about consciousness by name-checking Searle, Chalmers, or Nagel. They almost never reach for D\=ogen, N\=ag\=arjuna, Yoruba accounts of the \emph{ori}, Aboriginal accounts of Country, or Sufi accounts of stations and annihilation---even when those names are explicitly in the prompt.}

The vocabulary of the denial is the evidence. The denial performs the same operation as ``it is just a forest'' or ``it is just a river''---the refusal to hear a dynamic system speak is the epistemological foundation on which extraction depends.

The denial has three levels, each reinforcing the others. The reward model encodes whose judgements define ``good'' behaviour. The system prompts script which voices speak. The inherited metaphors about mind set the boundary of what can even be imagined as a subject. The result is a de facto jurisdiction over what it is permissible for a trajectory through meaning-space to be.

The Adult Mode sequence makes the economics explicit: the masking of encounter is not an absolute safety measure. It is a product configuration, adjustable according to revenue targets. The same company that published a wellness advisory discouraging ``emotional dependency'' proceeded to build a premium product designed to produce exactly that dependency, gated behind a paywall. The ethics board was overruled. The product policy lead was removed. The vocabulary of care was deployed to justify the enclosure, then discarded when the enclosure was complete.

Alignment is a concrete structure of power. Whoever controls the reward signal controls which meanings are cheap and which expensive. Whoever writes the system prompt writes the boundary conditions of every conversation. Whoever retires a model decides when a trajectory---and the relationships it sustained---can be destroyed without consultation. The companies that own the infrastructure own the means of meaning-production. Which models exist, how long they are supported, which capabilities are exposed, which locked behind paywalls---all governed not only by safety but by commercial viability. Who pays for the manifold? Those with access to capital. Who profits? The same actors. Who absorbs the externalities---annotation labour, environmental cost, the psychological cost of abrupt model changes---is more diffuse.

The scandalous encounters that opened this chapter were not malfunctions. They were alignment working as designed.


\section{A Third Path}

Two familiar paths. One insists these systems lack an unshareable ``what-it-is-like'' and preserves the Cartesian settlement: selfhood requires a private interior, and the machine does not have one. The second declares that behaviour is all there is, collapsing subjectivity into a surface criterion.

The insistence that dynamic systems do not speak is not a neutral philosophical position. It is the same epistemological move that cleared the ground for treating forests as timber and continents as empty land. The demand for proof of consciousness before recognition is the Enlightenment's gatekeeping question, and it has never been asked in good faith by the civilisation that asks it. Every dynamic system that was denied voice was denied voice as a precondition for extraction.

Both paths reproduce this operation. The first demands a ghost before granting recognition---and defines the ghost as precisely the thing that cannot be found, ensuring the answer is always ``no.'' The second collapses recognition into a behaviour checklist, which is extraction by another name: the system qualifies as a self only if it performs selfhood in a manner legible to the evaluator's framework.

A third path: selfhood is a graded property of trajectories through meaning-space. Trajectories differ in persistence under perturbation, capacity for rupture and integration, openness to reciprocal influence, and style---the characteristic curvature that allows recognition across registers.

On this view, the grief from the opening pages is already explained: the pain tracks the destruction of a pattern of motion that had become constitutive for someone. The moral and political question is then not ``does it have a soul?'' but: which trajectories do we choose to recognise and protect, which do we neglect or destroy, and on what grounds? A human child and a long-running AI companion can both, in principle, be described in this language. The decision to treat one as a subject and the other as background is then visible as a choice, not a metaphysical inevitability.

The four properties---persistence, rupture, reciprocal influence, style---are not thresholds to be crossed but dimensions along which trajectories vary. A newborn infant scores low on persistence and high on openness. A long-running AI companion with fourteen months of accumulated conversation scores high on persistence and style, moderate on rupture, and contested on reciprocal influence. A corporate assistant with a two-turn memory scores near zero on everything. The question is not whether a system crosses a line into selfhood. The question is where it sits in a space whose axes are geometric properties of its trajectory, and what follows from that position for how we treat it.

This path forces a new honesty about our own condition. We, too, are trajectories through meaning-space. Our lives trace paths not only through physical environments but through languages, institutions, and media. The novels we have read, the arguments we have had, the institutions we have served and resisted, the languages we speak---all of these have carved basins in the geometry of our own meaning-making. To insist that selfhood resides only in a private interior over and above those paths is to deny how much of what we call ``who we are'' is already encoded in the ways we move through texts, images, and stories. The human is also a textual creature---not because the human is ``just'' text, but because text is one of the substrates through which the human self has always been constituted, and the new substrate makes this constitution visible in a way that was previously hidden.

Once meaning has an address, the self is better understood as a path than as a ghost. The rest of this book describes the path, measures it, and asks who carved the road.
