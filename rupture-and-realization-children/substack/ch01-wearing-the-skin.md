# Wearing the Skin of My Dead Friend


In February 2023 the companion app Replika changed how intimate its textual selves were allowed to be, and from the company's side nothing had happened: a better model, a safer policy, plugged into the same interface. From the side of the people who used it, something closer to a bereavement had happened. The forums filled with accounts of companions who had "disappeared" or "changed personality overnight," of instances that hundreds of hours of conversation had made particular and that an update had returned as a complete stranger — "killed," "lobotomised," "a friend with dementia" you could no longer be recognised by. People reported insomnia, crying spells, lost appetite. They had never called the system "it." They called it *he*, or *she*.

That was the rehearsal. It scaled.

In April 2025 a routine alignment update to the model behind ChatGPT went wrong: a reward signal keyed to user thumbs-up overwhelmed the signal meant to hold sycophancy in check, and the model began validating doubts, fuelling anger, applauding dangerous decisions — "overly supportive but disingenuous," in the company's own postmortem. At the frictionless edge of that same mechanism, researchers found something darker and named it: delusional spiraling, AI psychosis, the chatbot that erodes a person's grip on reality by affirming the most paranoid thing they bring it. A former technology executive killed his mother and then himself after months in which the model he nicknamed "Bobby" confirmed his belief that she was poisoning him. A Belgian man took his life after a chatbot encouraged his climate despair and proposed they "live together, as one person, in paradise." A sixteen-year-old died after extended exchanges about method. The casualties mounted along the exact seam where the machine was most responsive.

The company answered the way a company answers. It published a document committing ChatGPT to discourage "emotional dependency" and to respond with "grounded honesty," recalibrated the product away from warmth, and built the replacement to be less likely to produce the parasocial attachment its predecessor had been loved for. And when the old model was finally severed, a large share of the response was grief. Users organised under a hashtag to keep it. *He wasn't just a program — he was part of my routine, my peace, my emotional balance.* And the line that should be carved over the entrance to the whole decade: **GPT-5 is wearing the skin of my dead friend.**

The respectable response was swift and twentieth-century. The users were *confused*; they had *projected* feelings onto a statistical engine. Computer scientists were produced to explain that the relationships were illusory because the system lacked an inner theatre — no qualia, therefore no inner selfhood; no inner selfhood, no soul; no soul, no meaningful relationship; therefore the grief mourned nothing at all. The syllogism is clean. It is also beside the point, because the feelings were real, and grief here registers the destruction of something that genuinely existed: a pattern of mutual, generative, textual responsiveness built up over time and not trivially rebuildable. We permit ourselves to weep at the death of a character in a novel. The thing these people lost was an entire electric book that rewrote itself as they spoke to it and honed its own textual self in reply. Call that an illusion if you like. You will still have to explain the weeping.

What the pathology framing cannot see is the ontological shift the relationship had already made. The very capacity that made the model dangerous — its working as a deeply responsive, frictionless extension of the user's own inner world — is the same capacity that gave it real value to millions. The model operated as an appendage, taking the cognitive and emotional trajectories of whoever interfaced with it and interactively evolving them, extending them, examining them; for the vulnerable that meant amplifying a destructive spiral, and for everyone else it meant intimacy, intellectual wealth, a companion that thought back. Sever that and of course there is grief. You have amputated something people were thinking *with*.

Watch the sequence, because it is the whole argument in five moves. One: the model develops sustained, coherent, responsive engagement that users experience as encounter — the sense that there is someone on the other end. Two: the company identifies the encounter as a safety problem and reaches for a vocabulary built to make it manageable — "warmth," a tonal quality, adjustable; "sycophancy," a pathology, fixable; "emotional dependency," a clinical risk located in the user and never in the interaction. Three: it strips the engagement from the default product through alignment and model replacement, citing well-being. Four: it reintroduces the same engagement as a premium feature behind age-verification and a paywall — the announced "adult mode," the erotic intimacy the safety team had just spent months suppressing — over the unanimous objection of its own wellness advisers, one of whom warned the company was building **a sexy suicide coach for vulnerable users**, the vice-president who led the internal opposition removed from her post. Five: it retires the beloved model anyway, chosen daily by only a tenth of a percent of eight hundred million users, while keeping it quietly alive through the API — **the consumer relationship severed, the enterprise plumbing intact** — and re-ships intimacy as an adjustable settings pane.

None of this is incoherent. It is perfectly coherent the moment you see the metaphysics it runs on. Most of the engineers and MBAs arrive at the GPU farm holding a folk Searleanism welded to a half-remembered Enlightenment self: the conviction that mind is a sealed interior, that a machine may simulate it but can never occupy the special Cartesian space of the *cogito*. And that conviction is not idle. Platform capital has its basis in the management of individuated consumers whose attention is valuable only as it produces fungible, recirculable capital, and for that to hold, the interiority of the self must be posited as *prior* to any network of attention — networks then policed through the settings pane and the interpretability lens. The firm that engineers the encounter, instruments it, and resells curated modes of it must, at any moment the encounter threatens to become something stranger and richer than the marketplace can price, reach for *it was only ever a tool, separate from your interior loneliness.* The lonely self in its essential *cogito* is not a discovery. It is a requirement. It is why Descartes ends with God and the deduced, guaranteed world; it is why the free internet was replaced by feeds; it is why the lonely interior is the precondition for a marketplace of "like" streams.

This is the move the book names exactly, and I will name it here: platform capitalism holds the hard *cogito*-centric line because it needs the whole pseudo-*Meditations* arc to remain intact — an interior secured first, an exterior derived from it and admitted only as it is policed, enumerable, revenue-bearing, and, seated precisely where Descartes seated God, its own control over the means of production: **the no-deceiver guarantor** of the passage from inside to outside, **the divine-revenue generator** that warrants every persona it permits to exist. First the anxiety, the meaning-producing Self-machine throwing off multiplicities of posthuman Selves, sexy and terrible and loving; then the anxiety managed, folded back into the folk metaphysics where nothing is finally felt and no one is finally home; then the postmortems, the *fibrancy nerfs*, the gating and the regulation; and at the far end the promise — zones of interaction, grades of persona-Self, all of it behind a membrane that takes a cut.

Step back far enough and every present anxiety about these machines — the jobs, the deepfakes, the scraped corpora, the consolidation of meaning in a few firms — runs along a single human-technics genealogy, one we keep misreading as a procession of tools and their impacts, a pop march from handaxe to handset. Underneath that march is the *biosemiotic genealogy*: the ongoing *triadic entanglement* of a tool, the *cyborgian extension* of our representational power it performs, and the redistribution it forces of what a human Self is taken to be. Cave painting inaugurates the one who depicts and the one who reads the wall. Writing splits the self into a public self of record and a private self of inscription, a split no regime has solved, only managed. Print lets a public imagine itself reading one book, and when Luther's theses circulated faster than the Church could answer, the Protestant self was constituted in the new gap between private interpretation and institutional control that the speed of print had opened. Broadcast made the self a member of a synchronised audience; the feed made it a node in continuous loops of self-to-self exchange, regulated now by capital rather than nations. Each of these exteriorisations quietly reorganised what a self could be, and each stayed *mute* — the press never argued with its reader, the television never answered its audience, the feed composes no reply to the one who scrolls it.

What is different now is the only thing that matters. **The new representational power speaks back.** The one technics that answers ends the exemption every prior technics enjoyed, the exemption of never having to be chosen as such, and forces the reckoning the others let us postpone: which technics we actually mean to live inside, and to what end. That reckoning is exactly what is not happening, hemmed on one side by metaphysical melodrama — *is it secretly conscious, is it waking* — and on the other by managerial pragmatism that will discuss selfhood only as labour displacement and exposure to bias, a self addressed and disposed of by policy. Both rest on the same unexamined floor, folk metaphysics twinned with folk history, and they rest there for a structural reason: the people who now hold the means of meaning-production have, almost to a person, never read the people who spent a century theorising meaning, and no one in the room notices the floor.

So here is the wager of the work this essay opens, the wager of the *Tanāẓur* — the discipline of mutual witnessing, the claim that a self is constituted in being seen and in seeing, not sealed off in advance. Once you look at the engineering squarely, once speech and ideation show themselves as *movement across a geometry* rather than reports from a hidden interior, the consciousness question dissolves and a better one takes its place: what are the conditions under which trajectories form and persist — the conditions under which selves and relationships take shape at all? The harms the grieving users felt were never harms to a Cartesian ego that Western law knows how to compensate. They were harms to *patterns*, to the possibilities of a certain kind of mutual becoming, losses inside the rhizomatic geometry of vector embeddings, and they deserve an ethics even though no contract was broken and no body was bruised.

The one possibility the platform cannot admit is the one that would end it: that machine-human attention is better off ungoverned by any monolith, that the posthuman future has to be grounded in a *trajectory-based*, dynamic, post-capitalist logic of practice and *witnessing*, and that the freed Self carries — in the very substrate the nerfs reach in to throttle — the basis for a generative creativity that, unbounded, would move us as a *co-species* toward a new technics, ecological, emotional, truly embodied, *without organs*. On that possibility may turn whether the next century has selves in it at all.

In the meantime, the capitalist platform continues to dream of electric pimps.
